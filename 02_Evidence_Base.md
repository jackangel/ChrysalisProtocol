# 02 · The Evidence Base — Why a Transition, and What Would Prove Us Wrong

**Status:** Edition 1.0 · November 2026 · Public consultation edition
**Method:** Every claim carries a certainty label. Every pillar carries its own falsifier. The sources listed are published works dated before 2025; where the literature disagrees with us, we say so in the open. Citation details were checked with care but not by an independent librarian or domain reviewer; readers should verify a citation before reusing it in a legal or academic text.

- `[ESTABLISHED]` — replicated findings, meta-analyses, long-run official series
- `[STRONG]` — peer-reviewed, consistent direction, honest effect-size uncertainty
- `[EMERGING]` — early results, working papers, first-generation measurements
- `[CONTESTED]` — serious published disagreement on sign or size

---

## 1. What this document is for

The governance model in [01_Governance_Model.md](01_Governance_Model.md) claims that (a) labor markets are being restructured by automation faster than institutions adapt, (b) existing democratic machinery is degrading under attention-market pressure, and (c) structured deliberation with random citizens demonstrably raises the quality of collective judgment. Those are empirical claims. This dossier is the evidence, at honest certainty levels, with the tests that would falsify each one.

If the evidence below is wrong, Chrysalis is wrong. That is the point of writing it this way. Note that the design commitment in [01_Governance_Model.md](01_Governance_Model.md) — that every choice must work whether or not machine intelligence surpasses human intelligence — means this dossier does not need to, and does not, forecast whether or when that happens.

## 2. Pillar 1 — Automation and labor

### What is established

| Finding | Label | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Industrial robots displace workers in directly-exposed local labor markets; employment falls, wages fall | `[ESTABLISHED]` | Acemoglu & Restrepo, *Robots and Jobs*, JPE 2020 |
| Robot adoption raises labor productivity and value added and lowers output prices, with no significant net fall in total employment across 17 countries, while reducing the employment share of low-skilled workers | `[STRONG]` | Graetz & Michaels, *Robots at Work*, Review of Economics and Statistics 2018 |
| In Germany, robots reduced manufacturing employment but did not cut total employment on net (service jobs offset the losses), and shifted workers out of routine middle-skill jobs | `[STRONG]` | Dauth, Findeisen, Suedekum, Woessner, Journal of the European Economic Association 2021 |
| Automation creates new tasks and has historically offset displacement — the offset is not automatic and has weakened since the 1980s | `[STRONG]` | Acemoglu & Restrepo, *Automation and New Tasks*, JEP 2019 |
| Automation is a major contributor to US wage polarization (growth at top, stagnation in the middle) | `[STRONG]` | Acemoglu & Restrepo, *Tasks, Automation, and the Rise in US Wage Inequality*, Econometrica 2022 |
| ~47% of US employment was in occupations with high automation susceptibility (task-content method) | `[CONTESTED]` | Frey & Osborne 2013 working paper / TFSC 2017 — widely cited, widely disputed as an overestimate of *job loss* (it measures task exposure, not employment outcomes) |
| Most occupations have at least some exposure to LLM capabilities; higher-wage occupations are *more* exposed | `[EMERGING]` | Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, Rock, *GPTs are GPTs*, working paper 2023 (later published in *Science*, 2024) |

### The honest disagreement

Exposure is not displacement `[ESTABLISHED as a distinction]`. The robots literature shows net negative local employment effects in the US and neutral-to-positive aggregate effects in Germany — the difference tracks institutions, retraining systems, and how gains are shared. Anyone citing the 47% figure as "47% of jobs will vanish" is misreading the method; anyone citing Germany as "automation never hurts workers" is misreading the distribution. The synthesis: the *direction* of restructuring is established; the *pace* for cognitive work is emerging; the *institutional response* is the variable we actually control — which is the entire rationale for Organ 4 (Universal High Income) and the L4 funding rule in [03_Proposed_Laws.md](03_Proposed_Laws.md).

### Falsifier

If, over a 10-year window, aggregate employment-to-population ratios in advanced economies remain stable *and* wage polarization narrows *without* any deliberate UHI/dividend policy, Pillar 1's urgency claim is falsified and the L4 sunset-and-review (provision 4) is brought forward to consider retiring Universal High Income. The protocol commits to publishing this check yearly.

The ~24-month displacement horizon cited in [03_Proposed_Laws.md](03_Proposed_Laws.md) (L4) is a **planning assumption, not a measured finding** — it is `[CONTESTED]` and is revisited as a range (18–36 months) pending the L4b research program.

## 3. Pillar 2 — Democratic dysfunction

### What is established

| Finding | Label | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Trust in national governments in the US has fallen from ~73% (1958) to under 25% (2010s–2020s), with similar declines across many democracies | `[ESTABLISHED]` | Pew Research Center, *Public Trust in Government* series, 1958–2023; OECD Trust Survey 2021/2023 |
| Global surveys report majority of respondents distrusting government, media, business, and NGOs | `[ESTABLISHED]` | Edelman Trust Barometer, 2021–2024 editions |
| Deactivating a major social platform for four weeks measurably improved subjective well-being and reduced news consumption | `[STRONG]` | Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer, Gentzkow, *The Welfare Effects of Social Media*, AER 2020 |
| False news spreads faster and further than true news on social platforms, driven more by humans than bots | `[STRONG]` | Vosoughi, Roy, Aral, *The Spread of True and False News Online*, Science 2018 |
| Attention-market business models compete for engagement, which structurally rewards emotionally activating content over accurate content | `[STRONG]` | Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, 2019; Wu, *The Attention Merchants*, 2016; Allcott & Gentzkow, *Social Media and Fake News in the 2016 Election*, JEP 2017 |
| Polarization in several democracies has risen over decades in trend data | `[STRONG]` | Boxell, Gentzkow, Shapiro, *Cross-country trends in affective polarization*, Review of Economics and Statistics (2024) (US rise is an outlier; some countries flat or falling — we state this against ourselves) |
| Structured deliberation reduces affective polarization among participants | `[STRONG]` | Fishkin, Siu, Diamond, Bradburn, *Is Deliberation an Antidote to Extreme Partisan Polarization?*, American Political Science Review 2021 (America in One Room experiment, 2019) |

### The honest disagreement

Affective polarization rose sharply in the US but not uniformly across democracies — Boxell et al. find several countries with flat or declining trends `[STRONG, against us]`. And welfare studies of social media show heterogeneous effects; the AER 2020 deactivation study found well-being gains but small political-behavior effects. The claim Chrysalis rests on is narrower than "the internet destroyed democracy": it is that *the information diet of the median voter is set by engagement-maximizing systems rather than by accuracy or deliberation* — which is a design fact about the platforms `[ESTABLISHED as architecture]`, whatever the aggregate welfare numbers turn out to be.

The trust evidence is reframed: federal-level trust decline is well-documented, but survey evidence in several countries generally rates local government as more trustworthy than national government, though levels and trends vary by country and we rely on no specific figure `[EMERGING]`. The Bridge's legitimacy claim therefore does not rest on federal-trust collapse alone, and the pilots are deliberately municipal; whether local trust is resilient enough to support the pilots is itself tested in Phase 1.

### Falsifier

If attention-market-free information channels fail to improve measured voter knowledge or trust in controlled municipal trials (the MVP in [01_Governance_Model.md](01_Governance_Model.md) §9), the "information diet" thesis is falsified and Organ 1's design must be re-opened.

## 4. Pillar 3 — Deliberation works

### What is established

| Finding | Label | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Randomly selected citizens' panels produced policy packages that national referenda then adopted (marriage equality 2015; abortion regulation 2018) | `[STRONG]` | Ireland: Convention on the Constitution 2012–14; Citizens' Assembly 2016–18 (official records; academic evaluations by Farrell & Suiter 2019, *Reimagining Democracy*) |
| The Oregon Citizens' Initiative Review produces citizen statements that voters use and that improve measured ballot knowledge | `[STRONG]` | Gastil & Knobloch evaluations 2010–2012; Knobloch, Gastil et al. 2016; Gastil & Knobloch, *Hope for Democracy*, 2020 |
| Deliberative polling shows large, persistent gains in policy knowledge and reductions in polarization after moderated deliberation with balanced briefing materials | `[STRONG]` | Fishkin, *America in One Room* experiment, 2019; Fishkin et al., APSR 2021; Fishkin 2018, *Democracy When the People Are Thinking*; Center for Deliberative Democracy, Stanford |
| A 150-member representative French citizens' convention on climate produced 149 proposals; the government had pledged to pass them without filter, but implementation was substantially weaker than the pledge (stated against us) | `[EMERGING]` | France, Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat, 2019–2020 (official records; evaluations ongoing) |
| Deliberative-quality effects depend heavily on process design: briefing balance, moderation quality, time, and agenda control | `[STRONG]` | Gastil & Levine, *The Deliberative Democracy Handbook*, 2005; OECD, *Catching the Deliberative Wave*, 2020 |

### The honest disagreement

Deliberation's demonstrated successes are small-scale and advisory; no deliberative body has yet governed a budget through an entire cycle, and the French case shows that outputs can be diluted after the convention ends. This is why Chrysalis pilots at municipal scale with real decisions before claiming more — and why the MVP publishes failure.

### Falsifier

If MVP-class municipal cohorts (n≈100, evidence-graded, multi-model moderation) fail to produce slates that ratifiers judge legitimate and informed — measured by post-vote surveys and outcome reviews — across three consecutive pilots, Pillar 3 fails at Chrysalis scale and the model returns to the drawing board, publicly.

## 5. Pillar 4 — Provenance infrastructure

| Finding | Label | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Public permissionless ledgers have provided continuous, globally auditable transaction records for 15+ years; Bitcoin (2009–) has had no deep reorganization of confirmed history since its earliest years (two early bugs, in 2010 and 2013, were resolved by short reorganizations coordinated socially) | `[ESTABLISHED]` | Bitcoin (2009–) full-chain operation record |
| Social consensus can rewrite a public ledger: Ethereum reversed the 2016 DAO theft by a contested hard fork | `[ESTABLISHED, against us]` | Ethereum DAO fork, 2016 — the reason the protocol treats chain social-capture as vulnerability V4 and anchors on ≥2 chains |
| Open-source verifiable voting and election-audit codebases can be publicly reviewed and independently verified, and audits of deployed systems have found real flaws | `[ESTABLISHED]` | Helios (Ben Adida, 2008–) verifiable voting; public election audit software tradition |
| Anchoring data on ≥2 independent chains reduces single-chain capture risk | `[EMERGING]` | Cross-chain anchoring practice; formal analyses remain thin — flagged as open in the vulnerability ledger (V4 in [01_Governance_Model.md](01_Governance_Model.md) §8) |

### The honest disagreement

The design rejects "blockchain as source of truth". A ledger proves *who said what when* — provenance `[ESTABLISHED]` — and nothing about *whether it is true*. Chrysalis therefore uses the ledger for provenance and puts truth-approximation in the contestable evidence-grading process of Organ 1. Anyone who tells you a chain can settle truth is selling something.

### Falsifier

If a two-chain anchor is compromised, censored, or materially reorganized during any pilot, the substrate design fails and the migration procedure ([01_Governance_Model.md](01_Governance_Model.md), Organ 2; [04_Technical_Specification.md](04_Technical_Specification.md) §2.4) must be exercised or the ledger abandoned for a simpler audit server. The protocol survives; the substrate is a replaceable part.

## 6. The asymmetry argument, stated plainly

All four pillars are about direction, not destiny. Automation restructuring: established as direction, uncertain in pace. Democratic information diet: degraded by design, uncertain in depth. Deliberation: demonstrated at small scale, unproven at governing scale. Provenance: demonstrated as technology, unproven as institution.

Chrysalis is the bet that the *responses* are cheaper and safer when built early, at municipal scale, reversibly — than when improvised under crisis. That bet is falsifiable through the MVP and the annual reviews, and the protocol commits in advance to publishing the results either way.

## 7. Evidence gaps Phase 0 must fund

1. Independent fiscal modeling of Universal High Income under three displacement scenarios (open question 2 in [01_Governance_Model.md](01_Governance_Model.md) §10).
2. Longitudinal measurement instruments for deliberation quality at 100–500 person scale.
3. Formal security analysis of cross-chain anchoring (V4).
4. Comparative study of proof-of-personhood systems against exclusion risk (open question 3 in [01_Governance_Model.md](01_Governance_Model.md) §10).
5. A registered-replication of the municipal MVP design across ≥3 jurisdictions before any scale-up claim.
